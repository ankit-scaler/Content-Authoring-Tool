title: Comments
description:
duration: 300
card_type: cue_card

Now, let's talk about comments. Show why some of the statements are not giving any output and ask them to find the differences.

Comments -> Statements which we want our compiler to ignore. 
Comments are of two types :
1. Single Line Comments : To write a single line comment just add two forward slashes (//) in front of the statement.
// System.out.print("HI"); // This is a single line comment.

2. Multi Line Comments : In order to comment out multiple lines together, we add /* at the beginning and then */ to close the comment.
/* System.out.print("Hello"); This is a multi line comment. */

title: History Repeats The Pattern of Every "Scary" Tool
description: Calculators, Excel, Google, and now AI
duration: 180
card_type: cue_card

History Repeats Itself

Every time a powerful new tool arrives, people panic. And every time, the same thing happens — the tool raises the floor, and skilled workers become more valuable, not less.

Walk through the diagram aloud with the class:
- Calculator (1970s): People thought accountants were done for. Reality? Accountants stopped doing tedious arithmetic and started doing analysis — and got paid more for it.
- Excel (1990s): "Bookkeepers are finished!" Instead, anyone who could use Excel became a business analyst. The job evolved.
- Google (2000s): "Why hire researchers when anyone can search?" Researchers became 10× faster — and the value shifted from finding information to judging which information is right.
- AI (today): Same story, new chapter. AI is the latest tool. It's not replacing developers — it's separating the ones who learn it from the ones who don't.

Key line to land with confidence:
"AI doesn't replace developers. It replaces developers who refuse to learn it."

title: What AI Is Actually Good At (and Where It Fails)
description:
duration: 240
card_type: cue_card

Let's Be Honest About AI

AI is genuinely powerful at some things — and genuinely terrible at others. The skill is knowing the difference.

🤖 Where AI is genuinely strong today
- Writing working code fast — from simple functions to entire small features, AI can produce code that runs.
- Explaining things to you — "What does this line do?", "Why am I getting this error?", "Explain this concept like I'm 10." It's like having a patient tutor available 24/7.
- Generating first drafts of almost anything — code, test cases, documentation, even rough designs for a small app.
- Translating between languages — give it Python, get Java. Give it an idea in English, get code.
- Speeding up repetitive work — the boring, predictable stuff that used to eat hours.

Be honest with the class: "Yes — AI can write more than just boilerplate. It can build real things. That's exactly why the next part matters so much."

🧠 Where AI still fails — and this is YOUR territory
- Understanding what to actually build — AI gives you what you asked for, not what you needed. If your instruction is vague or wrong, the output will be confidently wrong.
- Subtle bugs that look correct — code that runs, looks clean, and quietly gives the wrong answer in one specific case. (We'll see a live example in a few minutes.)
- Knowing when its own answer is wrong — AI doesn't "know" it made a mistake. It will explain a wrong answer with the same confidence as a right one.
- Connecting the dots across a real system — in a real project there are dozens of files, rules, and decisions. AI sees only what you paste in. It can't see the full picture you're working inside.
- Accountability — when something breaks at 2 AM in production, your manager calls you, not the AI. The person who clicked "accept" owns the code.

Reframe to share with learners:
"AI is no longer just a typing assistant — it's a powerful collaborator. But a collaborator still needs someone in charge who knows what 'right' looks like. That someone is you. Companies aren't hiring 'people who can't code.' They're hiring people who can code AND use AI well. Developer + AI > AI alone > Developer alone."

title: Why Fundamentals Matter MORE Now, Not Less'
description: The most important slide in this entire section
duration: 240
card_type: cue_card

AI has changed how we write code, but it hasn't changed how software works.

Today, anyone can generate code using AI. The real differentiator is no longer typing syntax quickly — it's knowing what to build, why it works, and whether the generated solution is actually correct.

Imagine asking AI: "Solve this problem."
It may generate code in seconds.
But how do you know:
- Is the algorithm correct?
- Is it optimal?
- Will it pass edge cases?
- Can it handle millions of users?
- Why did it choose this approach over another?

Without fundamentals, you're forced to trust the AI blindly.
With DSA knowledge, you can verify, improve, and confidently use AI as a productivity tool instead of treating it as a black box.

That's exactly what programming fundamentals and DSA teach.

title: How We''ll Use AI in This Course
description: Setting healthy norms early
duration: 60
card_type: cue_card

So… should YOU use AI while learning?
Short answer: Yes, but as a tutor, not as a code-writer.

✅ Healthy use
- "Explain why this line gave me an error."
- "What's the difference between print and println?"
- "I've tried for 30 minutes — here's my code, can you give me a hint?"

❌ Not healthy (in the learning phase)
- "Solve this assignment for me." → You won't learn, and you'll fail interviews.
- "Generate the answer without me thinking." → You're cheating your future self.

The rule we'll follow this whole course:
First, you struggle. Then you ask peers. Then you ask AI for hints. Then — only then — for the full answer. The struggle is where learning happens. AI shortcuts the struggle and steals the learning.

Now — let's go become the kind of developer that AI works for, not against.

title: AI Coding & AI Probing - Platform Feature Introduction
description:
duration: 400
card_type: cue_card

🤖 Instructor Note: Introduce AI Coding & AI Probing
Instructor: Take a few minutes at an appropriate point in the session to introduce students to two AI-powered features now available on the platform — AI Coding and AI Probing. Keep this brief and awareness-focused; the goal is for students to know these exist, understand what they're for, and feel encouraged to use them.

What is AI Coding?
AI Coding is a feature where the platform provides learners with a code snippet or a problem statement to work with, and the learner engages directly with the AI around it — explaining, debugging, optimizing, or implementing as required. It is split into four modes:
- Explain the Code — Learners are given a piece of code and need to explain it to the AI - covering what the code does, how it works, and the logic behind it.
- Debug the Code — Learners are given a piece of code containing a bug and work through it with the AI to identify and fix the issue.
- Optimize the Code — Learners are given a piece of working code and discuss it with the AI to improve it further (better time/space complexity), explaining their reasoning.
- Implement the Code — Learners are given a problem statement and write the solution from scratch on their own, then walk the AI through their approach.

Why AI Coding matters for students:
Explaining, debugging, optimizing, and implementing code covers the full range of what's expected in real-world coding work — not just writing code from scratch, but also reading, fixing, and improving code written by someone else. This builds a much deeper, more practical understanding of concepts than passive learning. It's also exactly what's tested in technical interviews — being handed unfamiliar code and asked to explain, fix, or improve it on the spot, alongside writing fresh solutions. Practicing this regularly helps students build a well-rounded skill set, so they walk into interviews and real-world coding tasks more prepared and confident.

What is AI Probing?
AI Probing is a feature where, after a learner submits a solution, the AI asks short follow-up questions about their own code — for example, asking them to explain a specific line, walk through the logic, or justify a choice made in their approach.

Why AI Probing matters for students:
- It verifies that the learner genuinely understands the solution they've submitted, rather than having copy-pasted it from an external source.
- If a learner struggles to answer basic questions about their own submitted code, it's a useful signal — both to the learner and to us — that the concept needs more practice.
- It reinforces the habit of understanding code deeply before submitting it, rather than treating submission as the finish line.

Instructor tip: Frame this as a helpful checkpoint, not a "gotcha." Encourage students to treat AI Probing as a way to self-verify their understanding, similar to explaining a concept out loud to check if it truly makes sense.

In summary: AI Coding helps learners build hands-on fluency with reading, fixing, improving, and writing code, while AI Probing ensures that understanding stays genuine and not superficial. Together, these tools are designed to make learners better prepared — for assessments, interviews, and real engineering work.

Instructor: If students have further questions about either feature, direct them to Support or the relevant FAQ document.
Please make sure students are aware of both features before moving on.
